## 01. The collapse of image scarcity

For decades, photographs held cultural power because they were difficult and expensive to produce. As Susan Sontag observed in *On Photography*, pictures felt like reliable pieces of reality. If an image looked exceptional, it meant someone invested serious craft, time, and budget to capture something that actually existed.

Today, images are infinite and practically free. Even when a physical product is rare, digital feeds reproduce it across millions of screens within hours. When feeds are flooded with endless imagery, visuals alone stop being special. They become background noise.

---

## 02. The death of the subculture buffer

Cultural cool used to depend on isolation and time. Subcultures developed in local scenes: skate parks in California, punk basements, underground clubs. That gap gave people space to build shared rituals, specialized slang, and genuine community ties before outside businesses noticed.

Social algorithms eliminated that buffer.

The moment an aesthetic begins to surface, algorithms broadcast it globally. Instead of a living culture, it gets reduced to a photogenic costume:
- Skateboarding gets stripped of physical risk, leaving behind oversized jeans and canvas sneakers.
- Punk sheds its politics, surviving only as distressed band t-shirts.
- Wealth gets flattened into the "old money" look, ordered online in two days.

When any aesthetic can be copied as a fast-fashion outfit overnight, clothes lose their ability to signal real belonging.

---

## 03. Status moved from single items to whole routines

Because a single luxury item can easily be knocked off, rented, or copied, owning one special bag or jacket no longer proves good taste.

As Highsnobiety noted in their study *The Status Economy*, status shifted away from individual fashion pieces into daily routines: artisanal groceries, natural wine, boutique gyms, and niche reading lists.

To signal taste today, buying a jacket is not enough. Credibility comes from the consistency of your entire routine: what you eat, where you travel, and what you read. Taste is judged across a lifestyle, not bought in a shopping bag.

---

## 04. AI and the loss of photographic trust

AI image models broke the last piece of trust in photography: the assumption that a camera recorded a real physical event.

When anyone can generate photorealistic scenes in seconds for pennies, visual spectacle loses its punch. Our default reaction to a striking picture has flipped from admiration to suspicion: *did this actually happen?*

This poses an existential problem for traditional advertising. Commercial campaigns used to work by inspiring desire through aspirational photography. When viewers know an image is computer-generated or staged with infinite digital tricks, the fantasy no longer works.

---

## 05. Communities over campaigns

With polished images cheap and trust in pictures eroded, the traditional marketing playbook is broken.

| Dimension | Old Image Economy | What Works Now |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Main Asset** | The high-budget ad campaign | An engaged, active community |
| **Audience Role** | Passive spectator | Active participant and contributor |
| **Visual Role** | The main product of desire | Simple documentation of real events |
| **Source of Trust** | Production values and glossy polish | Long-term support and real involvement |

In the old model, the ad campaign was the prize, and people were just spectators to capture. Today, the community is what matters.

The photos a brand puts out are no longer the destination. They are simply honest records of real activities and shared commitments. Creating clean pictures is easy. What remains rare and valuable is patient, long-term support for real people and ideas.
