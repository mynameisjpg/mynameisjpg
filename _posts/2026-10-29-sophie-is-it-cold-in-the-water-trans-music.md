## 01. Freezing, burning, evaporating: from SOPHIE to ANOHNI and Moses Sumney

The first time I heard SOPHIE's *"Is It Cold in the Water?"* on *OIL OF EVERY PEARL'S UN-INSIDES* back in 2018, it felt like standing barefoot on a sheet of metal at the edge of an ocean. Up to that point on the record, SOPHIE had been throwing hyper-compressed latex, industrial pistons, and bubblegum snares at the listener. Then the drums dropped out. What remained were towering, shimmering synthesizer swells and Cecile Believe's voice suspended in the air, asking one question four times in a row.

> *"I'm freezing / I'm burning / I've left my home / Soft ache, me / Earth shaking / I feel alone / Is it cold in the water?"*

Anyone who has ever stood at the threshold of a bodily or existential transition recognizes that physical contradiction right away. Your skin feels too thin for the room, yet flushed with heat. To step outside the body, role and name you were and keep being assigned, to keep the peace for everyone else- Anyone who left home with no guarantee that the water below would hold their weight.

In SOPHIE's original arrangement, the synthesizers behave like physics engines. She built every sound on that track from raw waveforms on an Elektron Monomachine, sculpting electricity until it sounded wetter than actual water. By the final verse, the voice stops hesitating on the cliff. It jumps:

<div class="responsive-video" style="display: flex; justify-content: center;"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RxyT8PRSDRo" title="SOPHIE - Is It Cold In The Water?" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe></div>

> *"I'm swimming, I'm breathing, evaporating / Is it cold in the water? / I'm liquid, I'm floating into the blue."*

The fear of drowning gives way to a state change. The body stops being a solid, heavy target that society can police, and turns into mist and light.

When Moses Sumney and ANOHNI recorded their cover of *"Is It Cold in the Water?"* for the *TRAИƧA* compilation in 2024, three years after SOPHIE died in Athens reaching for the full moon, the song's gravity shifted completely. Sumney and ANOHNI stripped away the blinding digital sheen. In its place, they built a slow, nocturnal liturgy of Rhodes piano, low brass, and trembling vocal harmony.

Hearing ANOHNI's contralto wrap around Sumney's weightless falsetto broke something open in me. Sung by ANOHNI and Sumney, the song became a séance. SOPHIE had already evaporated into the blue, and the elders and kin she left behind were standing at the shoreline, calling across the water to ask her what it feels like on the other side.

ANOHNI called SOPHIE's original a "mistresspiece." When ANOHNI sings *"I've left my home,"* she carries forty years of queer grief in her throat, stretching back to the New York underground of the 1990s, to Marsha P. Johnson and Sylvia Rivera, and to the chamber mourning of her own work. Two generations of trans and non-binary vanguard music meet inside those four minutes.

## 02. Questioning my own ear: the dolls and the conchas

There is an old pattern in my own listening habits. Whenever I fall obsessively in love with a musician, a producer, or a sonic world, almost without exception, the artists who rewire my nervous system turn out to be either iconic **dolls** (trans women like **SOPHIE**, **Arca**, **ANOHNI**, and **Wendy Carlos**) or what queer slang affectionately calls **fish** or **conchas** (cis women and femme avant-gardists who perform an almost feral, hyper-embodied femininity).

Why does my ear keep hunting for this specific axis? What am I actually looking for when I put on headphones at two in the morning?

Part of the answer sits in how both groups relate to the materiality of the body and the voice. Most mainstream music treats the human voice as a natural, transparent window into a stable self: a guy with an acoustic guitar singing about his feelings as if his body and his social role were facts of nature. I have never trusted that transparency. It bores me, and on a deeper level, it feels dishonest about how human beings are actually put together.

The mutants never take the body for granted.

When I listen to **Arca** stretching a reggaeton dembow rhythm until it sounds like tendons snapping, or pitching her voice from a bruised baritone confession into an operatic shriek on *KiCk i*, I am hearing someone build a body in real time. Nothing is factory-default. Femininity, monstrosity, seduction, and vulnerability are treated as materials you have to forge with your own hands under high pressure.

And when I return to **ANOHNI**'s *"Cut the World"*, I hear the political reason why this vocal frequency matters so much to me:

## 03. The underground laboratory and the pop pantheon

Once you start paying attention to this lineage, the official history of popular and electronic music looks upside down. We are used to a narrative where mainstream cis icons sit at the center of culture, occasionally inviting an underground producer into the studio for a trendy remix. The historical record says the exact opposite: trans women have repeatedly built the instruments, the syntax, and the emotional architecture that mainstream pop only learns to speak years later.

Go back to 1968. Before **Wendy Carlos** released *Switched-On Bach*, the Moog modular synthesizer was widely viewed by the audio establishment as a novelty box for generating weird laboratory bleeps and radiophonic sound effects. Working closely with Robert Moog, Wendy suggested crucial hardware improvements, including touch-sensitive keyboards and portamento controls, and painstakingly multitracked Bach inventions line by line. She proved that voltage-controlled oscillators could breathe, phrase, and ache like an orchestra. Then she scored Stanley Kubrick's *A Clockwork Orange* and *The Shining*. Every synth-pop record of the 1980s and every modern electronic film score lives inside a house Wendy Carlos wired while navigating her transition in a hostile, voyeuristic media climate.

Four decades later, the same dynamic played out with **SOPHIE** and **Arca**.

In the early 2010s, big-room EDM and commercial pop had calcified into predictable four-on-the-floor drops. Working from underground club nights and PC Music releases like *"Bipp"* and *"Lemonade"*, SOPHIE treated plastic, artificiality, and hyper-feminine consumer aesthetics with total seriousness. People initially misread her early work as irony or satire of pop music. In truth, she was stripping away pop's rockist shame and showing what it could sound like when it stopped apologizing for being synthetic.

The biggest cis icons in the world heard it immediately:

- **Madonna** brought SOPHIE in to co-produce *"Bitch I'm Madonna"* in 2015, injecting the metallic, rubberized bounce of the queer underground straight into the bloodstream of legacy pop royalty.
- **Charli XCX** met SOPHIE and found the collaborator who unlocked her entire artistic second life. From the *Vroom Vroom* EP in 2016 through *"Out of My Head"* and *"Tears"*, SOPHIE gave Charli the vocabulary of hyperpop: a space where a club track could be simultaneously abrasive, glossy, and heartbroken. When Charli XCX conquered global culture with *BRAT* in 2024, its emotional center was *"So I"*, a quiet, devastated apology and thank-you note to SOPHIE. The entire *BRAT* phenomenon grew out of soil SOPHIE tilled.
- **Arca**, meanwhile, became the secret engine behind the most daring pivots of the 2010s. **Kanye West** pulled her into the *Yeezus* sessions in 2013 to tear holes in his production; **FKA twigs** built *EP2* and *LP1* with her; **Björk** co-produced *Vulnicura* and *Utopia* alongside Arca, crediting her with helping stitch her heart back together after a devastating breakup.

Trans women did the heavy structural engineering in the dark so that pop music at large could have a future.

## 04. From "Another World" to a "Whole New World": bedrooms, compilers, and learning to touch

In 2008, **ANOHNI** released *"Another World"*, a devastating piano elegy that begins with a quiet confession of exhaustion: *"I need another world / This one's nearly gone."* She sings of mourning the sea, the snow, the bees, and the things that grow, grieving an earth and a social order that have become uninhabitable. Ten years later, **SOPHIE** closed *OIL OF EVERY PEARL'S UN-INSIDES* with *"Whole New World/Pretend World"*, answering ANOHNI's grief with nine minutes of seismic, piston-driven synthesis: *"I looked into your eyes / I thought that I could see a whole new world."*

Between ANOHNI's plea for *Another World* and SOPHIE's eruption lies the entire secret of trans creation. SOPHIE's title pairs *"Whole New World"* with *"Pretend World"*. Before another world can exist in the streets or on a club floor, you first have to build it as a pretend world behind a closed bedroom door.

That bedroom is the real answer to the question **Jen Ives** asks in her video, *"Why Are Trans Women So Good At Electronic Music?"*. Anyone who spends five minutes in queer internet culture knows the double stereotype: *the trans girl who is either a wizard with a modular synthesizer and Ableton Live, or a senior systems programmer maintaining critical open-source infrastructure in Rust or C++*. Usually both. Underneath the affectionate jokes about "programming socks," ThinkPads, and Eurorack cables, Jen Ives names the raw human condition that produced this archetype: **isolation and loneliness**.

<div style="display: flex; justify-content: center;"><iframe data-testid="embed-iframe" style="border-radius:2px" src="https://open.spotify.com/embed/track/2rN1ODOsaNfYu782rw36jR?utm_source=generator&si=d82a6acc93e946d1" width="100%" height="152" frameBorder="0" allow="autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; fullscreen; picture-in-picture" loading="lazy"></iframe></div>

**A compiler and a synthesizer do not care what your body looks like.** In a digital audio workstation or a code editor at two in the morning, nothing has a fixed biological destiny. A sawtooth wave can be modulated into a flute, sheet metal, or a new throat; a programming language offers a universe where every error has a trace and any architecture can be rewritten from scratch. 

That exact same bedroom incubator produced **ContraPoints** (Natalie Wynn) and **Philosophy Tube** (Abigail Thorn). I remember watching both of their YouTube channels in the mid-2010s before either of them had transitioned, back when online discourse was overrun by grim "skeptic" talking heads lecturing into webcams about biological determinism. Wynn and Thorn started in that same sparse bedroom format, answering bad-faith arguments with academic philosophy.

These audio works of art let you be a beginner in your own skin. SOPHIE took the solitary math of her Monomachine and turned it into a `whole new world` where ten thousand strangers could sweat, cry, and hold each other in the club. And whenever I hear ANOHNI and Moses Sumney ask `"is it cold in the water?"`, I think of every kid still sitting alone in front of a glowing screen tonight, twisting a filter knob in the dark, getting ready to jump into the blue.
