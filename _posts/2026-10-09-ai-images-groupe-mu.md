## 01. The Trap of Verbal Thinking: Why Images Are Not Sentences

When scholars first started analyzing images with semiotics in the mid-twentieth century, they usually fell into one of two traps. One was traditional art criticism, which leaned heavily on personal intuition, poetic adjectives, and vague feelings. The other was what Groupe µ, a collective of Belgian linguists and semioticians founded at the University of Liège, called `linguistic imperialism`.

For decades, critics tried treating images like written sentences. They borrowed terms like _syntax_, _grammar_, and _vocabulary_, forcing them onto brushstrokes and photography without stopping to ask whether pictures actually function like speech.

Spoken and written language relies on arbitrary symbols. A picture works on our visual system directly. Light hits the eye, and the brain immediately starts extracting edges, contrast, and depth.

In their 1992 book _Traité du signe visuel_ ("Treatise on the Visual Sign"), [Groupe µ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupe_%CE%BC) built a framework made specifically for visual perception. Their core insight was that any image operates on two distinct channels: the **iconic sign** and the **plastic sign**.

That distinction cuts right to the heart of generative AI. Image generators like Midjourney, Stable Diffusion, and FLUX have mastered the plastic side of an image, while remaining almost completely blind to the iconic one.

## 02. Biological Vision as an Active Filter: Contrast, Texture Noise, and Gestalt Toggling

Groupe µ grounded their model in Gestalt psychology and neurophysiology. Seeing is not a passive recording device, like a camera sensor saving raw pixels. It is an active filtering process.

Before we ever recognize what we are looking at, our eyes and visual cortex do lower-level work. Cells in the retina use lateral inhibition to sharpen edges, isolate borders, and group nearby shapes. The brain quickly organizes those raw signals using basic spatial rules: grouping items by proximity, closing broken lines, and separating figures from the background.

Two perceptual habits stand out here. First, our eyes are tuned to the subtle roughness of the natural world. Real surfaces have irregular, fractal noise; when an artificial texture is unnaturally smooth, something immediately feels off. Second, when our eyes meet an ambiguous scene, the brain does not hold multiple interpretations at the same time. It toggles back and forth, flipping between foreground and background, or between a flat pattern and an illusion of depth.

Any working visual theory has to start with these low-level perceptual mechanics.

## 03. The Iconic Sign: How Pixels Trigger Mental Prototypes

We usually think of an iconic image as something that simply looks like a real-world object. Groupe µ showed that resemblance alone is not enough to explain how pictures work. A minimal line drawing, a pixelated icon, and a photorealistic render can all point to the exact same creature.

To explain how recognition happens, they broke the iconic sign into three parts:

1. **The Signifier:** The physical marks on the canvas or screen (lines, colors, or pixels).
2. **The Referent:** The actual object or scene in the physical world.
3. **The Type:** The mental model or category stored in memory that connects the marks to the object.

A sketch of a dog does not work because black ink physically resembles an animal. It works because the lines activate the mental type "dog" in our minds. As long as key proportions and features trigger that category, recognition clicks, whether the drawing has five pixels or five million.

## 04. The Plastic Sign: How Color, Contour, and Grain Speak Before Recognition

Groupe µ's second major insight was showing that an image also speaks directly through its raw visual material, completely independent of whatever object it depicts. They called this the plastic sign.

Plastic signs do not ask what a picture represents. Instead, they hit the eye directly through three core dimensions:

| Plastic Dimension | Visual Element                           | Perceptual Effect                                        |
| :---------------- | :--------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Form**          | Lines, contours, geometry                | Sets boundaries, orientation, and shape grouping         |
| **Color**         | Hue, saturation, brightness              | Sets tone, splits figure from ground, creates contrast   |
| **Texture**       | Micro-patterns, grain, surface frequency | Creates tactile feel, surface density, and visual weight |

These elements register before conscious recognition ever kicks in. A wash of cobalt blue, a sharp diagonal stripe, or a patch of rough cross-hatching affects how we feel and where we look well before the brain decides it is seeing a coat, a wall, or a stormy sky.

Artists deliberately manipulate plastic signs through stylization. They might simplify forms into bare polygons like the Cubists did, limit a palette to high-contrast complementary shades, or swap out smooth gradients for deliberate hatching. Far from being simple decoration, these choices strip away visual noise so the viewer focuses on specific sensations.

## 05. Visual Rhetoric and Iconoplasticity: Breaking Conventions to Produce Meaning

Visual rhetoric is not about fancy rhetoric in the literary sense. It describes how an image bends or breaks expected visual conventions to create meaning.

Groupe µ explained this through a simple progression: a baseline norm or expected pattern (the _degree zero_), a deliberate break from that norm (the _deviation_), and the moment the viewer registers the surprise and interprets what it means (the _re-evaluation_).

A classic example is René Magritte's painting _Le Viol_ (_The Rape_). The outline of a woman's head is preserved, but her facial features are replaced by a female torso: breasts for eyes, a navel for a nose, and a pubis for a mouth. Magritte swaps the expected iconic parts while keeping the plastic silhouette intact. The plastic outline says "head," but the iconic content says "body." That friction between plastic expectation and iconic reality is visual rhetoric in action.

Groupe µ called this interplay **iconoplasticity**. The plastic and iconic layers can reinforce one another, compete for attention, or deliberately collide to create tension, irony, or unease.

## 06. The Architecture of the Frame: Where the Picture Meets the World

Groupe µ also took a close look at the frame (_le cadre_). They drew a clear line between a contour, which is an edge outlining a shape inside the picture, and the frame, which marks the boundary between the artwork and the rest of the room.

The frame is never just neutral glass or border tape. It governs how the image meets the outside world. An artist can let elements spill over the border into external space (_débordement_), pull the frame inward to tightly crop the scene (_bornage_), or make the border so thick that it competes with the central subject (_embordement_). In some cases, the frame becomes an iconic picture of its own, like the ornate marginalia in medieval manuscripts or the decorative borders of vintage postcards.

## 07. Plastic Engines, Iconic Blindspots: Why Diffusion Models Glitch on Physical Reality

When a diffusion model trains on millions of images, it is not building a world model. It does not learn mental concepts (_types_) or understand how three-dimensional objects exist in space (_referents_). What it learns is high-dimensional statistical correlations between text prompts and pixel distributions: lighting gradients, specular highlights, color palettes, and surface frequencies.

Because of this architecture, generative models behave as plastic rhetoric engines.

They excel at plastic execution. Volumetric mist, subsurface skin scattering, cinematic reflections, and intricate textiles look astonishing because diffusion models are extraordinarily good at matching local statistical textures and color palettes.

At the same time, they stumble on iconic coherence. Because the model has no concept of skeletal anatomy, mechanics, or gravity, it easily draws a person with three elbows, merges an umbrella shaft into a jacket, or casts shadows that run in opposite directions.

When you look at an AI-generated image, your early visual processing reacts to the plastic harmony first: the gorgeous warm lighting, the crisp depth of field, the rich textures. A second later, your higher-level cognition catches the iconic breakdown: the fingers melt into the teacup, or the reflection does not match the person standing before the mirror.

---

## 08. Diagnosing Synthetic Media: Moving Past the "Fake Image" Cliché

_Traité du signe visuel_ gives us a clear vocabulary for discussing synthetic imagery without getting bogged down in vague complaints about "fake pictures."

Current AI systems are masters of plastic composition. They orchestrate light, color, geometry, and surface with remarkable statistical skill, but they do so completely detached from the physical world.

By separating how an image acts on our eyes from what it actually represents, Groupe µ helps explain both the immediate allure of generative images and the uncanny cracks that appear the moment we look closer.
